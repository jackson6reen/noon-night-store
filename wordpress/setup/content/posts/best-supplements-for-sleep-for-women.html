Key takeaways

  • Match the supplement to why you can't sleep: a tense body, a racing mind, or a body clock that's off.
  • Low-dose melatonin plus magnesium glycinate covers the most common problems for the most people.
  • Skip 5-HTP and tryptophan if you take antidepressants.
  • Trouble sleeping most nights for three months or more is worth a doctor's visit.

Women are more likely than men to report insomnia, and hormones, stress and the mental load all play a part. The supplement aisle is overwhelming, so here's a plain-English guide to what each popular option actually does.

First: why can't you sleep?

  • "My body won't relax": tight shoulders, restless legs, cramps. Look at magnesium and glycine.
  • "My mind won't stop": replaying the day, to-do lists at midnight. Look at L-theanine, lemon balm and ashwagandha (for daytime stress).
  • "My timing is off": late nights, jet lag, shift work. Look at low-dose melatonin.
  • "I wake at 3 a.m.": see our guide to waking up at 3 a.m.

The supplements, one by one

Melatonin

What it does: Melatonin is the hormone your body releases in the dark to signal that it's night. As a supplement, it's best studied for falling asleep a bit faster and for resetting your body clock (jet lag, late schedules).

The catch: many store products are 5–10 mg, much more than studies suggest most people need, and higher doses are linked to next-day grogginess. Independent lab testing has found that the actual melatonin in some supplements varies widely from the label. Go low and choose a brand you trust.

Skip or ask first if: you're pregnant, nursing or trying to conceive, or you take blood thinners, sedatives, immunosuppressants, or diabetes or blood-pressure medication.

Magnesium (glycinate)

What it does: supports normal muscle relaxation and a calm nervous system. Many adults don't get enough from food. Glycinate is gentle on the stomach.*

Best for: women who feel physically tense at night, or whose sleep gets worse around their period. See Evenly Unwind.

Glycine

What it does: an amino acid studied (usually at about 3 g before bed) for subjective sleep quality and feeling less tired the next day. It may help by lowering core body temperature. Magnesium glycinate gives you a little glycine along with the magnesium.

L-theanine

What it does: an amino acid from tea that promotes a relaxed-but-alert state, without sedation. It's a good fit for a racing mind and is often paired with magnesium.

GABA

What it does: GABA is the brain's main calming neurotransmitter, but how much oral GABA reaches the brain is debated. Evidence for sleep is limited.

5-HTP and tryptophan

What they do: precursors your body uses to make serotonin and melatonin. Important: they can interact dangerously with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, triptans and other serotonin-affecting drugs. We don't sell them for that reason. Never combine them with antidepressants without your doctor.

Ashwagandha

What it does: an adaptogenic herb studied for everyday stress, cortisol and sleep quality, especially KSM-66 root extract. It works best taken daily for several weeks.* See Evenly Calm.

Skip or ask first if: you're pregnant or nursing, or have a thyroid, liver or autoimmune condition.

Valerian root and hops

Traditional sleep herbs, often combined. Research results are mixed. Some people find them helpful and others notice nothing. Valerian has a strong smell, and it shouldn't be combined with alcohol or sedatives.

Lemon balm, chamomile and lavender

Gentle calming herbs. Chamomile tea is a lovely wind-down ritual in itself. An oral lavender oil preparation has been studied for anxiety-related restlessness. Lemon balm is often combined with valerian.

Tart cherry (Montmorency)

A natural source of melatonin and antioxidants. Small studies of tart cherry juice suggest modest improvements in sleep time. Watch the sugar if you drink the juice.

Vitamin D, B6 and zinc

These support many body processes, including those involved in sleep, but they mainly help if you're actually low. Ask your doctor about a vitamin D test, especially in winter.

Where we'd start: The Sleep Stack: magnesium glycinate to unwind the body, plus a low-dose melatonin formula to signal sleep. It's the most common pairing for a reason.*

Supplements work better with these habits

  • Same wake-up time every day, including weekends.
  • Dim lights and screens for the last hour.
  • Cool, dark bedroom (about 65–68°F).
  • No caffeine after noon. Alcohol in the evening fragments sleep.

When to see a doctor

If you've had trouble sleeping three or more nights a week for three months, snore loudly, wake up gasping, or feel very sleepy in the daytime, please talk to a doctor. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, and sleep apnea is very treatable. More in our sleep guide.
