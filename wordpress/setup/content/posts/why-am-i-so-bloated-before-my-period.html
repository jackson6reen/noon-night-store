Key takeaways

  • Period bloating comes from hormone-driven water retention and slower digestion, sometimes both at once.
  • Less salt, more water and potassium, and a daily walk make a real difference.
  • Magnesium and digestive enzymes target both sides of the problem.*
  • Bloating that's there all month isn't "just your period." Get it checked.

You're not imagining it. Many women feel noticeably bloated in the days before their period: heavier, puffier, and with a stomach that feels inflated by the afternoon. Here's why it happens and what to do about it.

What causes period bloating?

1. Water retention

In the second half of your cycle, shifts in estrogen and progesterone affect how your body handles salt and water. The result is that you hold on to more fluid, which shows up as puffiness in your belly, fingers, ankles and breasts.

2. Slower digestion

Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle, including in your gut. Food moves more slowly, which gives gut bacteria more time to ferment it, and fermentation means gas.

3. Prostaglandins

Right as your period starts, prostaglandins (the compounds behind cramps) can also speed up your bowels. That's the familiar mix of cramps, gas and loose stools on day one.

4. Cravings

Salty, sugary cravings before your period are common, and those foods make water retention and gas worse.

What helps: food and habits

  • Cut back on salt in the week before your period. Most of it hides in packaged foods, takeout and bread.
  • Eat potassium: bananas, avocado, spinach, sweet potatoes and beans help balance sodium.
  • Drink more water, not less.
  • Skip fizzy drinks and gum, which add swallowed air.
  • Move: a 20–30 minute walk helps both water retention and gas.
  • Go easy on alcohol, which affects both fluid balance and sleep.

When bloating isn't just your period

Period bloating comes and goes with your cycle. See a doctor if your bloating:

  • is there most days, all month, for several weeks,
  • is new and persistent, especially if you're over 50,
  • comes with pelvic or belly pain, feeling full quickly, unexplained weight loss, or changes in bleeding or bowel habits.

Persistent bloating is one of the symptoms doctors check for ovarian cancer, celiac disease and other conditions. It's usually nothing serious, but it's worth checking. More in our bloating guide.
