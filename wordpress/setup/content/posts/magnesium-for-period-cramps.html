Key takeaways

  • Magnesium supports normal muscle relaxation, and small studies suggest it can help with period cramps.*
  • Glycinate is the gentlest, best-absorbed everyday form. Avoid oxide if you have a sensitive stomach.
  • Take it daily across your cycle, not just on day one.
  • Severe or worsening pain deserves a doctor's visit, not just a supplement.

If you've ever searched "what helps period cramps besides ibuprofen," magnesium has probably come up. It's one of the most popular natural options, and unlike many trendy supplements, there's actually some research behind it. Here's what we know, and what we don't.

Why magnesium might help cramps

Period cramps are caused mainly by prostaglandins, compounds released by the uterine lining that make the uterus contract. Magnesium plays a role in normal muscle relaxation and nerve signaling. The idea is simple: support the muscle's ability to relax, and the cramping may ease.

There's another angle too. Many US women don't get enough magnesium from food. Leafy greens, nuts, seeds, beans and whole grains are the best sources, and most of us don't eat them every day.

What the research says

A Cochrane review of herbal and dietary therapies for painful periods looked at several small trials of magnesium and found it was more effective than placebo for pain relief. The trials were small and older, so the researchers called for better studies. That's an honest summary: promising, low-risk, not yet definitive.

Magnesium has also been studied, sometimes alongside vitamin B6, for premenstrual symptoms like mood changes, water retention and bloating, again with encouraging but limited evidence.

How much and when to take it

  • Dose: studies have mostly used about 200–360 mg of magnesium a day. The National Institutes of Health sets the upper limit for supplemental magnesium at 350 mg/day for adults, unless your doctor recommends more.
  • Timing: evening works well for most women, because it doubles as part of a wind-down routine.
  • Consistency: take it daily across your whole cycle. Many women notice the difference after one or two cycles.

Who should be careful

Talk to your doctor first if you have kidney disease, are pregnant or nursing, or take antibiotics (tetracyclines, quinolones), bisphosphonates, diuretics or blood-pressure medication. Magnesium can affect how some of these are absorbed, so spacing doses a few hours apart is often recommended.

Other things that help period cramps

  • Heat: a heating pad on your lower belly is well studied and works.
  • Gentle movement: walking, yoga or stretching.
  • Omega-3s and ginger have also been studied for period pain.
  • Sleep: poor sleep makes pain feel worse. See our sleep guide.

When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your period pain keeps you home, isn't helped by over-the-counter pain relievers, is getting worse over time, or comes with very heavy bleeding, pain during sex or bleeding between periods.
