Key takeaways

  • Waking briefly at night is normal. Being wide awake every night usually has a cause you can change.
  • The usual suspects are stress, evening alcohol, a warm bedroom, blood-sugar dips and hormone shifts.
  • In the moment: no clock, no phone, slow breathing, and get up after 20 minutes.

You fall asleep fine, then you're suddenly awake at 3 a.m.: heart a little fast, mind instantly busy. It's one of the most common sleep complaints, especially among women. Here's what's usually behind it.

7 common causes of waking up at 3 a.m.

1. Stress and an early cortisol rise

Cortisol naturally starts climbing in the early morning to get you ready for the day. When you're stressed, that rise can come earlier and stronger, enough to pull you out of lighter sleep. Daytime stress support, like these approaches, can help at night too.

2. Alcohol

A glass of wine can make you fall asleep faster, but as your body processes it, sleep becomes lighter and more broken in the second half of the night. That's classic 3 a.m. territory.

3. Lighter sleep later in the night

Deep sleep happens mostly early in the night. Later cycles are lighter and richer in dream (REM) sleep, so you're simply easier to wake. Noise, light, a partner moving or a pet can tip you over.

4. A bedroom that's too warm

Your core temperature needs to drop for good sleep. A warm room or heavy bedding is a common, easy-to-fix cause. Aim for about 65–68°F.

5. Hormone shifts

Progesterone has a calming, sleep-supporting effect. When it drops in the days before your period, many women sleep more lightly. In perimenopause, night sweats and changing hormones make 3 a.m. waking very common.

6. Blood-sugar dips

A big sugary dessert, or going to bed hungry, can lead to a dip overnight that triggers stress hormones. A balanced dinner with protein, fiber and healthy fat helps.

7. Needing the bathroom

Cut back on fluids in the last 1–2 hours before bed. If you're getting up several times a night, mention it to your doctor.

What to do when you wake up

  1. Don't look at the clock. Doing the math ("only 3 hours left!") ramps up stress.
  2. Leave your phone alone. Bright light and notifications wake your brain up fully.
  3. Slow your breathing: in for 4 counts, out for 6–8, for a few minutes.
  4. Still awake after about 20 minutes? Get up, sit somewhere dim and read something boring until you feel sleepy.

Preventing it

  • Same wake-up time every day.
  • Alcohol earlier in the evening, or not at all on work nights.
  • A cool, dark, quiet bedroom.
  • A wind-down routine. Many women include magnesium glycinate to support relaxation through the night.*

When to see a doctor

If you're waking most nights for three months, if you snore loudly or a partner notices you stop breathing, or if you have night sweats, a racing heart or low mood, please talk to your doctor.
